# BSTS

## What model does
Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) models represent an observed time
series as a combination of latent structural components, regression effects,
and stochastic error.

## Assumptions

## 1. Stationarity is not required

BSTS does not require the observed time series to be stationary.

Instead, non-stationary behavior can be represented explicitly through structural
components such as:

- local level
- local linear trend
- seasonal effects

The level is therefore allowed to evolve over time.

This makes BSTS potentially useful for revenue series with changing trends or
other forms of non-stationarity.

## 2. The structural components provide a reasonable representation of the data

BSTS assumes that the major systematic patterns in the series can be
represented by the components included in the model.

For example:

$$
y_t = \text{Level}_t+ \text{Trend}_t+ \text{Seasonality}_t+ \text{Regression}_t+ \epsilon_t
$$

If important components are omitted, the model may produce poor forecasts.

For example, a revenue series affected by a major policy change may require an
intervention or regression component rather than relying only on a local trend.


## 3. Structural components evolve according to stochastic processes

BSTS allows latent components to change over time, but their evolution is
assumed to follow specified stochastic processes.

The model therefore assumes that changes in the underlying level and trend are
stochastic but governed by the specified state-space structure.


## 4. Observation errors are typically modeled as conditionally normal

A common BSTS specification assumes:

$$
\epsilon_t\sim N(0,\sigma_\epsilon^2)
$$

This means that, conditional on the latent states and predictors, observations
are normally distributed around the model-implied value.

The normality assumption is primarily part of the probabilistic model and
facilitates Bayesian inference.

## 5. State disturbances are typically modeled as normal

The evolution of latent states commonly assumes Gaussian disturbances.

This means the model assumes that changes in the underlying level and trend
are generated by stochastic disturbances with specified distributions.

## 6. Regression relationships are reasonably specified

The regression component assumes that the relationship between the predictors
and the target can be reasonably represented by the specified regression
structure.

⚠️ Limitation - If the relationship is strongly nonlinear, the standard linear regression
component may not capture it adequately.


## 7. Exogenous variables must be available for forecasting

As with ARIMAX, future predictor values must be known or forecastable.

This is particularly important when using BLS or BEA variables.


## 8. Predictor relationships should be reasonably stable

Although BSTS allows the underlying time series to evolve over time, a standard
regression component generally assumes that the estimated relationship between
predictors and the dependent variable is reasonably stable.

For example:

$$
\beta_{Employment}
$$

is generally treated as a parameter whose uncertainty is estimated rather than
as a coefficient that automatically changes every period.

Structural changes may therefore require:

- intervention variables
- time-varying coefficients
- interaction terms
- separate modeling periods
- alternative model specifications


## 9. Priors should be reasonably specified

BSTS is Bayesian, so prior distributions are an explicit part of the model.

The posterior distribution is proportional to:

$$
p(\theta|y)\propto p(y|\theta)p(\theta)
$$

where:

- $p(\theta|y)$ = posterior distribution
- $p(y|\theta)$ = likelihood
- $p(\theta)$ = prior distribution

The priors should be sufficiently informative to stabilize estimation when
appropriate, but should not impose unrealistic assumptions on the model.

Prior sensitivity should be considered when results are strongly influenced by
the choice of priors.


## 10. Variable-selection assumptions should be considered

BSTS can use Bayesian variable-selection procedures when many predictors are
available.

For a predictor $x_j$, a variable-inclusion indicator can be represented as:

$$
\gamma_j\in\{0,1\}
$$

where:

$$
\gamma_j=1
$$

indicates inclusion of the predictor and:

$$
\gamma_j=0
$$

indicates exclusion.

The model therefore assumes that the available predictors contain a subset
that provides useful information about the target.

Highly correlated predictors can make variable-selection probabilities harder
to interpret.


## 11. No major unmodeled structural breaks

BSTS is more flexible than ARIMA when dealing with changing trends, but it does
not automatically solve every structural-break problem.

A major policy change can still require explicit modeling.

Examples for municipal revenue include:

- tax-rate changes
- introduction of a new tax
- elimination of a tax
- changes in collection procedures
- changes in accounting definitions
- major economic shocks
- changes in the underlying tax base

These may be represented using intervention or indicator variables.

For example:

$$
Revenue_t = \mu_t+ \beta_1PolicyChange_t+ \epsilon_t
$$


## 12. Residuals should contain little unexplained structure

After accounting for the structural components and regression variables, the
remaining residuals should ideally contain little systematic information.

Residuals should be examined for:

- autocorrelation
- changing variance
- remaining trend
- remaining seasonality
- systematic forecast bias

If substantial structure remains, the model specification may be incomplete.


## 13. Sufficient historical information is needed

Although BSTS can be flexible with relatively short time series, more complex
models require sufficient observations to estimate their components reliably.

This is particularly important when including:

- trend
- seasonality
- many predictors
- multiple structural components
- time-varying parameters

For a short annual municipal revenue series, a highly complex BSTS model can
easily become unnecessarily difficult to estimate.


# BSTS Diagnostic Checklist

After fitting a BSTS model, evaluate:

| Diagnostic | Desired result |
|:---|:---|
| Stationarity | Not required |
| Structural components | Appropriate for the data |
| Trend | Reasonably captures underlying trend |
| Seasonality | Captures meaningful seasonal structure |
| Regression effects | Economically plausible |
| Residual mean | Approximately 0 |
| Residual autocorrelation | Limited |
| Residual variance | Reasonably stable |
| Structural breaks | Explicitly modeled where appropriate |
| Predictor availability | Known or forecastable |
| Multicollinearity | Limited/manageable |
| Prior sensitivity | Results reasonably robust |
| MCMC convergence | Adequate |
| Effective sample size | Adequate |
| Posterior diagnostics | Acceptable |
| Out-of-sample performance | Competitive with alternatives |


# Important Difference from ARIMA

The fundamental difference is the way the models handle non-stationarity.

This makes BSTS particularly attractive when you believe the underlying
generating process itself changes over time.


# BSTS for Municipal Revenue Forecasting

For municipal revenue, BSTS may be particularly useful when a revenue stream
has:

- a changing long-term trend
- structural changes
- policy interventions
- economic predictors
- substantial uncertainty
- relatively complex dynamics

For example:

$$
SalesTax_t = \mu_t+ \beta_1RetailSales_t+ \beta_2Employment_t+ \beta_3Policy_t+ \epsilon_t
$$

where:

$$
\mu_t=\mu_{t-1}+\eta_t
$$

allows the underlying revenue level to evolve over time.
